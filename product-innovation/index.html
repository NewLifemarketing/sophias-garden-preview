Steps from constraint to locked process

Chocolate is the evidence, not the identity

Chocolate is where we started and where most of our volume sits, and it is a genuinely difficult medium to work in. The constraints we solve here — even distribution at low dose, stability on a shelf, and flavour that does not lean on sugar — apply to any format we take on. The extract side of the business runs on the same discipline.

Chocolate as a delivery system

Most people meet chocolate as a confection. In edible cannabis manufacturing it is a carrier — a fat-based medium that cannabinoids can be dispersed into. That makes cocoa butter chemistry the starting point of the formulation rather than a finishing touch.

Why cocoa butter in particular

Cocoa butter has an unusually narrow and well-defined melting range. That is what gives chocolate its snap at room temperature and its clean melt in the mouth, and it is also what makes it a predictable medium to formulate into. Predictability is the most valuable property a carrier can have.

The tempering constraint

Cocoa butter can crystallise into several different forms, and only one of them produces good chocolate. Tempering is the controlled cycle that encourages that form and suppresses the others. Introducing a cannabinoid extract changes how the fat system behaves, so the formulation and the tempering have to be developed together rather than one after the other.

The dose problem

A 1 mg unit sounds simple and is the hardest thing to make reliably. The cannabinoid has to be distributed uniformly through the entire batch so that every unit moulded from it carries the same amount. Uneven distribution does not average out — it produces units above and below the declared figure, which is a compliance failure as well as a quality one.

How a format gets developed

  1. 1

    Define the constraint

    Format, dose, the cannabinoids involved and the flavour direction are fixed first. Everything downstream serves them.

  2. 2

    Build the extract

    The cannabinoid is incorporated into the cocoa butter phase rather than added to finished chocolate.

  3. 3

    Temper and trial

    The formulation is run through a full tempering and moulding cycle, because a recipe that works in a beaker and fails on a line is not a formulation.

  4. 4

    Verify by analysis

    Laboratory testing confirms the declared figures on an actual production lot.

  5. 5

    Lock the process

    The parameters that produced the result become the documented process for that product.

A formulation is not finished when it works once. It is finished when it can be reproduced, and demonstrated.

What we develop for

Format, dose accuracy, stability over time, and flavour that does not depend on additional sweetness. Those four constraints decide most of what a product becomes.

Questions

Common questions

Why chocolate rather than a gummy or a capsule?

Cocoa butter has an unusually narrow and well-defined melting range, which makes it a predictable medium to formulate into. Predictability is the most valuable property a carrier can have when the target is an accurate low dose.

Do you make anything other than chocolate?

Yes. XTRACT, under our Infyn Extracts brand, is a compressed oral tablet with no fat phase at all. The constraints we solve in chocolate — even distribution, shelf stability, flavour that does not lean on sugar — apply to any format we take on.

What is tempering, and why does it matter here?

Cocoa butter can crystallise into several forms and only one makes good chocolate. Tempering is the controlled cycle that encourages that form. A cannabinoid extract changes how the fat system behaves, so formulation and tempering have to be developed together.

Why is a 1 mg dose hard to make?

Manufacturing tolerance is proportional rather than absolute. An error that is invisible on a 10 mg piece is ten times as significant on a 1 mg one, so a smaller declared figure demands a tighter process to hit the same accuracy.
