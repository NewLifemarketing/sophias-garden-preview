Reading a certificate of analysis for an edible product

The COA is the document behind every figure on a package. What it reports for an edible product, what each section is confirming, and how a retailer should read one.

If the label is the summary, the certificate of analysis is the source. A COA is the laboratory record for one specific lot, and it is where the declared figures come from. For a retail buyer it is the single most informative document a producer can hand over.

A COA belongs to a lot

The most important thing to understand is that a COA describes a lot, not a product line. Asking for “the COA for Micro Zen” is not quite the right question — the right question is which lot you are being offered, because the results are specific to that production run.

Every certificate identifies the lot number, the licence holder, what was submitted, the date of analysis, and the laboratory that performed it. Analytical testing is carried out by laboratories licensed by Health Canada for that purpose.

Cannabinoid content

For an edible the cannabinoid section is reported per unit as well as per package, because the unit is what a person actually consumes. For a product declared at 1 mg of THC per unit, this is the section that confirms the declaration is accurate.

This is also where the value of a measured format becomes visible on paper. A tight result against the declared figure is evidence that the formulation and the process are doing what they are supposed to do.

Safety screening

The larger part of any certificate is the part nobody markets: confirmation that the product does not contain what it should not. Depending on the product and applicable requirements this can include screening for pesticides, heavy metals, microbial contamination and mycotoxins, and — where extraction is involved — residual solvents.

These are reported as results against limits. A compliant product sits within the permitted limits across every one of them. It is unglamorous, and it is the substantive part of the document.

Reading one as a retailer

Check the lot number first and confirm it matches what is in front of you. Check the date of analysis. Confirm the per-unit cannabinoid figures against the label. Then read the screening sections and confirm everything is within limits.

If you range a product repeatedly, keep the certificates. Across several lots they tell you something a single certificate cannot: how consistent a producer actually is. That is a far better measure of a manufacturer than any individual result.

Certificates of analysis for current lots are published to the retailer portal alongside the rest of the product documentation.
