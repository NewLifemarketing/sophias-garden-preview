How edibles are different: format, timing and why precision matters

An edible is not an inhaled product in a different wrapper. The route through the body is different, and that difference is the entire argument for measured, low-dose formats.

A great deal of confusion about edible cannabis comes from assuming a milligram is a universal unit. It is not. The number on a package describes a quantity of a compound, and how that compound is delivered changes what the number means in practice.

Two different routes

Anything inhaled reaches the bloodstream through the lungs, which is a short and direct path. Anything swallowed takes a longer one: through the digestive system, then through the liver, before entering general circulation. Those are different mechanisms with different timing.

The practical consequence most people notice is delay. The digestive route takes substantially longer than inhalation to produce any effect at all, and how much longer varies between individuals and depends on what else has been eaten. An edible does not announce itself on the same schedule.

Why the delay makes precision non-negotiable

Here is the part that matters for manufacturing. With a delayed response, a person cannot easily judge what they have taken in the moment. They are relying entirely on the declared figure and on the assumption that the next unit matches the last one.

That reliance is a responsibility that sits with the producer. If the units in a package vary, the person consuming them has no way to know. This is why we consider dose accuracy a safety-adjacent manufacturing obligation rather than a quality nicety, and why our formats are built around it.

The case for low dose

It is also the clearest argument for low-dose formats. A 1 mg unit is a small, predictable increment. Ten of them in a package means the format itself gives an adult granular control over what they are doing, rather than presenting a single large unit as the only option.

Health Canada caps an edible package at 10 mg of THC. Both of our products carry that full 10 mg, divided into ten units of 1 mg. The regulation sets what a package may contain; the format decides how finely that is portioned, and a 1 mg increment is a deliberate manufacturing choice rather than a lower total.

What we will not tell you

You will notice this article does not describe how any of this feels. That is deliberate and it is not evasion. A licensed producer in Canada may not make claims about effects, and beyond the legal position there is a practical one: individual responses vary, and a manufacturer is not in a position to characterise them.

What we can tell you is exactly what is in the product, how much of it, how it was verified, and how the format is constructed. That is the information a person actually needs from the company that made it.
